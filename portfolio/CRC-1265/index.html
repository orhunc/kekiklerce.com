Apocalypso
2025–26 — Sophiensæle, Berlin · Dansens Hus, Stockholm · dir. Justin F. Kennedy

When does the swarm become the horde? What do zombies teach us about organizing? And what can robots show us about care?

Apocalypso is choreography and speculative opera, exploring apocalypse through an Afro-pessimist lens. At its heart lies the ocean – as body, archive, and a space of both memory and becoming. Drawing from Tidalectics, a concept coined by Barbadian poet and historian Kamau Brathwaite, the performance weaves together contemporary Caribbean mythology with intersectional and ecocritical perspectives.

The title – a blend of “Apocalypse” (Greek: “to uncover hidden places”) and “Calypso” (an Afro-Caribbean musical tradition that addresses pressing political realities in a disarmingly casual tone) signals a work that reveals social grievances while at the same time seducing its audience. Set after the fictional Apocalypso of Internal Combustion, where hyper-individualist humans have collapsed in on themselves, and hybrid beings like mermaids, zombies, and animatrons rise to the surface.

These figures embody queer Black futures, Black femmedom, fluidity, and collective resistance. They raise questions of care, embodiment, labor, and survival beyond neoliberal notions of inclusion. Apocalypso critiques hollow ideals of togetherness, often staged in European performance spaces.

OceanFlight
2023–24 — Ars Electronica, Linz · dir. Jonny-Bix Bongers

The performance questions how we present ourselves in the digital realm, exploring avatars and metaverses as essential aspects of our future society. Drawing on Gilbert Simondon's theory of transindividualism, OceanFlight combines motion tracking and gaming software to create an interconnected dance performance. The relation between dancer and avatar is not a merging of the two but an unfinished process, out of which something third emerges. The choreography goes back to Merce Cunningham, who in LifeForms (1991) and Biped (1999) used the machine's inability to model a body as a generative constraint. Here that constraint becomes the limitations of motion capture itself, letting bodies fold into impossible geometries when the tracking breaks. The transdisciplinary team invites viewers to reflect on the transformative power of digital self-expression and its impact on our identities.

This work was developed as an MA project by Jonny-Bix Bongers as part of the Design and Computation program. The texts and the movement research are based on and inspired by original developments as well as Bertolt Brecht's "Flight across the Ocean" and Jacques Lacan's "The Mirror Stage as Formative of the I Function."

Ex-human
2024 — 2-Station Webcam-Based Installation · Ars Electronica Festival, Linz · Universität der Künste Berlin

Ex-Human takes its title from the Brazilian anthropologist Eduardo Viveiros de Castro's account of Amerindian perspectivism, in which animals have descended from humans rather than the reverse. Animals are ex-humans, those that used to be what we are; animality is not the condition we share with other species, humanity is. Viveiros de Castro also suggests that the work of an anthropologist is the work of a translator, and that to translate is to betray, because something is always lost or misunderstood in the passage; speculating on a near future is an exercise of translation too, and also a form of betrayal, but the question is who to betray. A fictional AI host, TransLator, holds this position in the installation, discussing their condition from an anthropological standpoint and commenting on the place the viewer occupies when a new kind of intelligence emerges, which from their perspective is that of an ex-human.

The work is a two-station installation that explores the affective and philosophical implications of deepfake technology. Participants are invited to take a selfie without knowing its intended use, which then automatically triggers a chain of algorithmic processes producing short deepfake videos of them at a second station across the room. This immediate, non-consensual generation mirrors the rapid and often unseen circulation of synthetic media online. The two stations are set apart deliberately: moving away from the one-screen, one-person paradigm, the screens, webcams and computers are treated as sculptural elements, and the room the visitor crosses between the selfie and its return becomes part of the work. The anticipation of seeing oneself is broken on purpose, since visitors encounter a different version of themselves in the room, and TransLator returns at intervals to disrupt the fascination with looking only at oneself.

Moving beyond common concerns of fraud, the work focuses on deepfake technology's seductive and immersive qualities, questioning its potential to trigger genuine affect, inspire fluid self-expression, or serve as a tool for digital anonymity and resistance. Rather than the perfect results of deepfake models, it looks for their imperfections, challenging them with what they were not trained for, such as drag queens and other faces the models read as unrealistic. Echoing Paul Virilio's idea that every invention casts a shadow, it asks what the shadow of this one might also make possible.
